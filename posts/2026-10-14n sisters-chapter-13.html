Book 4: Grace filename: Sisters Chapter 13 description: Grace tells of her first years in Kansas City: missing Carrie, Woodland School, outings with Aunt Julia, and her start as a nursing student in 1900. *************** ##Chapter 13: Life in Kansas City Despite the loving attention of my grandmother and aunt, my move to Kansas City was traumatic in many ways. First of all, I missed Sam and Martha, but I missed Carrie even more. She and I had been the best of friends and we had done everything together. Every day I woke up and remembered that Carrie was no longer with me. I arrived in February during the winter months and it was cold. The streets were muddy or frozen; every walk to school or any ride in a carriage meant that you needed a warm coat and a blanket over your lap. Although South Dakota was also cold, Sam and Martha had not had a great deal of money to buy me a lot of clothes, especially warm clothes. I learned much later that Sarah Etta had sent money regularly for my support, but Samuel and Martha did not want me to have more than Carrie. So they tended to just put it away and hope that they would some day give it to me. Aunt Julia took me shopping and we got some warmer clothes for me. I was glad to have those clothes, but I still found Kansas City very noisy and dirty. Aunt Julia let me stay with her and I appreciated that, but my aunt and my grandmother told me almost immediately why I had been brought to Kansas City. I remember discussions about wills and my Aunt Sarah Etta, about her accident and the pain she suffered. But it was quite a while before it dawned on me that Sarah Etta might have been my mother. woodland-school.jpg ds right 350px Woodland School, at the corner of Eighth and Woodland (from a 1908 history of Kansas City) My aunt had already enrolled me in school before I had even arrived, so within a few days she took me to the school — Woodland School — and introduced me to the teacher. I hated being the “new girl” in the school, but after a while I felt more comfortable with it. I didn’t make many friends because I usually arrived just in time for school and would walk home immediately after. I had strict instructions to not go anywhere else, but that was fine with me. I learned the walking route to school and back, and I never varied that. When I first got there, they tested me to see how well I could read and do my numbers. I was very good at both and they were immediately willing to put me into Grade 5. I was somewhat overwhelmed by the number of students in the class. I kept to myself, worked hard, and got good grades all the time. My life was very regimented: awaken and eat breakfast with Aunt Julia, walk to school, attend classes, walk home, do my schoolwork until Aunt Julia got home, and then we would have some supper together and it was time for bed. I was all right with this schedule, simply because I didn’t have to think about it: I just did it. kansas-city-public-library.jpg ds left 350px The new Public Library at Ninth and Locust, opened in 1897 (from a 1908 history of Kansas City) As the months and years went by, I did get more used to the city, and Aunt Julia took me to many places: the Kansas City festival, Fairmount Park, Swope Park, and Buffalo Bill’s Wild West Show. My favourite thing to do was read, so I loved the new public library that opened in 1897. I had to admit that there were a lot of things to see and do in Kansas City and, as I got older and more comfortable with living with Grandma and Aunt Julia, I began to enjoy it just a little bit. I wrote a few letters to Samuel and Martha, but they rarely responded and, in time, our communication was very limited. Aunt Julia got married, but I remained living with her and I got to know James Alan Ham, her husband, a little bit. But, generally speaking, I preferred to be by myself, even after I left elementary school and went to high school. By the end of the century, I was finished with my public school education and decided I wanted to become a nurse. My grandmother, who was married and was now living in Richmond, Missouri, and my aunt (also married) strongly encouraged me to find a way to take care of myself without a husband. Becoming a nurse appealed to me and I was definitely interested in staying in Kansas City for a little longer if possible — as well, a young man was courting me. In early 1900 I was a nursing student at Buena Vista Hospital for Women. I lived with other nursing students and with the patients we were to care for. I felt that I had finally found my footing.

Author’s Note: The women in Sisters were real people, and I have told their story as accurately as I can for lives lived in the 19th century. I can prove and support many of the facts with records from the time. But no record can tell me exactly what someone said, and for some events I know only portions of the story. So I have also had to use my imagination, to put you, the reader, inside the head of each woman as she tells her own story.
