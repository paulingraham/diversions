Book 4: Grace filename: Sisters Chapter 14 description: Grace tells of her nurse’s training in Kansas City, a private-duty job that took her to Caton, New York, her marriage to Bert Herrick, and the joys and sorrows of raising a family. *************** ##Chapter 14: Becoming a Nurse, Getting Married, Having a Family troost-and-11th-1895.jpg ds right 350px The northwest corner of 11th and Troost in 1895, four years before the hospital moved there (Missouri Valley Special Collections, Kansas City Public Library) By the beginning of the 20th century — 1900 — I was training to be a nurse. I lived at 1037 E. 11th Street, which was on the corner of Troost Avenue and East 11th Street. It was the Hospital for Women and Children, which had been founded in 1897, just three years before. The house itself was called Buena Vista, which means Beautiful View, but a lot of people named their houses back then, so knowing the name of the house wasn’t necessarily useful in finding it. The Hospital for Women and Children was a small, private hospital which had anywhere from 22 to 35 patients. The superintendent, the nurses, and the patients all lived there together. Our superintendent was a woman named Christiana Bauer, a 32-year-old widow who had been born in Michigan — but they changed routinely. There were ten nurses, including myself, and we came from many different states. At the time I was there in 1900, there were anywhere from 10-20 patients, usually women and some children. At the time of the Census, in June of that year, our youngest patient was a child of nine and our oldest patient was a woman of 50. Nursing training in Kansas City at that time was typical of nursing training anywhere in the country. The hospital was the school and I did not have to pay any fees. To be accepted, however, I had to take tests in basic subjects — math, English, science — and I had to have a letter vouching for my character. Many people asked their minister to do that, but I also had several teachers write letters for me. I was given room and board and a small monthly allowance for my uniforms and my books. I was not too crazy about the first stages of becoming a nurse because it was really basic training: making beds, changing linens, and cleaning and bathing patients. It usually took most of us two to three years to finish all of the requirements. Willa Cather described the routine in an 1897 magazine article: up at six, breakfast at half past, on the ward at seven, and on duty until seven at night. Students got one day off a week, or half a day when the hospital was busy. In her words, “the discipline is as rigorous as that in a convent school.” I could heartily agree with Willa Cather! As I advanced in my training, I was allowed to do other things, such as giving medicine and assisting doctors during surgery, or sometimes monitoring the meals that patients were having. In the evenings, there were lectures to attend (usually given by doctors). The topics included anatomy, medicines, and how to nurse surgery patients or new mothers. Sometimes we were sent out to work in private homes, but the hospital kept the fee, which I didn’t think was completely fair. However, I liked that kind of work and it would eventually help me to move on to a different sort of nursing. In June of 1900, my Uncle Samuel (the one who had raised me) was a patient at the State Hospital for the Insane near St. Joseph, Missouri. As a result, Martha moved down to the area so that she could visit Samuel as often as possible. Carrie had married in 1897 and was no longer at home, but Martha still had several children, all but one too young to care for themselves: Fred, 18; Pansy, 12; Alline, 8; Harvey Arthur, 4; and Stanley, 2. Grandmother Brand and Aunt Julia were helping them, just as they had helped me. By 1910, Samuel was finally released and they moved to Oklahoma; Fred, age 28, and Stanley, age 12, remained living with them. grace-brand-kansas-city.jpg ds left 300px Grace Brand in Kansas City I felt that becoming a nurse would somehow obliterate and maybe even compensate for my dark past. Even though I knew that Sarah Etta was my mother, I never revealed that to anyone. I continued to tell people (like census takers) that Samuel and Martha were my parents. I worked at that hospital for several years, but there came a time when I wanted to leave Kansas City and work independently. There were often recommendations for jobs on the bulletin board at the hospital, and one day I saw a request for a private nurse to take care of an elderly lady in Caton, New York. Getting away from Kansas City appealed to me, even though I was engaged to a young man who was an osteopath. He was not happy that I decided to take the job and, in fact, not long after I moved to Caton, he called off the engagement and I threw my engagement ring down the outhouse hole! bert-herrick-portrait.jpg ds right 250px Bert Herrick In Caton, I became the nurse for an elderly 77-year-old lady, a woman named Electra Herrick, the daughter of Pierce Herrick and Ann Miller. Her nephew, Bert Herrick, dropped by often to visit his aunt and as time went by he began to take me out on buggy rides. Since my engagement was broken, I felt no allegiance to my so-called fiancé back in Kansas City and I willingly went on those rides with Bert. herrick-brand-wedding-1909.jpg ds left 300px The wedding announcement in an Ottawa newspaper, December 1909 Eventually Bert asked me to marry him, and I agreed. My Aunt Julia, who had married in Kansas City previously, now had a home in Canada in Ottawa, Ontario. Bert and I decided we wanted to get married there, so that Julia’s husband could give me away. We were married on the 8th of December in 1909. I was 30 years old while Bert was 35. It was a small wedding at Julia’s home on Lyon Street, and the Reverend Dr. Cameron of the First Baptist Church married us. I wore a princess-style gown of lace over ivory silk and carried a bouquet of roses and lilies of the valley. After dinner, Bert and I left for a wedding trip to Montreal. I was glad to finally be married; I wanted to have children. We lived in a farmhouse located in Caton, New York. Although Bert owned a farm, he wasn’t really a farmer. I think, however, that he was a natural-born salesman and he sold lightning rods and insurance. So he was gone quite a bit. He purchased the first car in Steuben County — an Overland. People came from all around the county to have a look at that car! He was also on the Steuben County Board of Supervisors. helen-hazel-ted-herrick.jpg ds right 350px From left: Helen, Hazel, and Ted Herrick, a friend of Ted’s, and the friend’s sister or mother While he was busy with his multiple jobs, I stayed home and eventually three children came along: Helen, born in 1913; Hazel, born in 1914; and Ted, arriving in 1918. I loved my children but I didn’t find it easy to be a mother, and Bert was often gone so the responsibility often was mine to teach, discipline, and care for my three children. Sarah Etta’s will — the one that my aunt and grandmother broke — repeatedly talked about the evils of giving birth out of wedlock, so I was very happy that I had found a man to marry and we had had children legitimately. Still, it was not a passionate relationship. On 30 June 1929, my oldest daughter, Helen, collapsed as she was mowing the lawn. We carried her into the living room and had her rest on the couch while we summoned medical aid. She died later that afternoon and we never really knew what had happened except that she must have had some sort of a birth defect of her heart. Some years later, my son, Ted, got a young girl pregnant. Bert and I were horrified that he had done such a thing. We did not think this young woman was the right person for him, but I was also mortified that his child would be illegitimate. Bert and I told him that he would have to leave home. He eventually married the young woman on 18 July 1939, but my relationship with him was never the same. Bert died on 27 January 1946. He was 71 years old. At 66, I was once again alone: my husband and oldest daughter gone, my son now the black sheep of the family, and my youngest daughter, Hazel, married and living with her husband and two children. It was time for me, at last, to go out on my own. I knew that my nursing training would help me once again. And it did.

Author’s Note: The women in Sisters were real people, and I have told their story as accurately as I can for lives lived in the 19th century. I can prove and support many of the facts with records from the time. But no record can tell me exactly what someone said, and for some events I know only portions of the story. So I have also had to use my imagination, to put you, the reader, inside the head of each woman as she tells her own story.
